# Berlin Rent — The Mietpreisbremse Didn't Work

**A data story by Ravi Dronamraju · July 2026**

---

In June 2015, Germany introduced the Mietpreisbremse — a rent brake designed to cap new-lease rents at 10% above the local Mietspiegel. Berlin was one of the first cities designated as a "tight housing market." The promise: slow down runaway rent increases.

Ten years later, the data tells a different story.

## The acceleration

Berlin's official Mietspiegel — the government's own benchmark for fair rents — has tracked base rents since 2013. Before the Mietpreisbremse, rents grew at **4.5% per year**. After the law took effect, growth **more than doubled to 10.8% per year**.

| Period | Years | Annual Growth |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| Pre-Mietpreisbremse | 2013–2015 | **+4.5%/yr** |
| Post-Mietpreisbremse | 2015–2023 | **+10.8%/yr** |

If the pre-2015 trend had continued, Berlin's base rent today would be €8.91/m². Instead, it's €12.50/m² — **40% higher than the counterfactual**.

## What happened?

The Mietpreisbremse has three major loopholes:

1. **New builds are exempt.** Any apartment built after October 2014 can charge market rates. In a city adding ~20,000 units per year, that's a growing share of the market outside the cap.

2. **"Modernized" units are exempt.** If a landlord renovates — new bathroom, new kitchen — the unit is considered "comprehensively modernized" and the cap doesn't apply.

3. **The Mietspiegel itself rises.** The rent brake caps rents at 10% above the Mietspiegel. But if the Mietspiegel rises 10.8% per year, the cap rises with it. The brake is attached to a moving target.

## The two Berlins

There are really two rental markets in Berlin:

| Tenant type | Average rent | 
|-------------|-------------|
| Existing tenants (Zensus 2022) | €7.97/m² |
| New tenants (Immoscout24 asking) | €10.79/m² |
| **Gap** | **+35%** |

If you're an existing tenant, you pay €478/month for a 60m² apartment. If you move, you'll pay €647. That's €2,028 more per year — just for changing apartments. The Mietpreisbremse was supposed to close this gap. Instead, it widened.

## Who can afford Berlin?

At €2,450 median household income (net), the 30% affordability rule says rent shouldn't exceed €735/month:

| Apartment | Cold rent | With utilities | Affordable? |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| Studio (40m²) | €451/mo | €551/mo | ✅ Yes (22.5%) |
| 1-bedroom (60m²) | €676/mo | €826/mo | ⚠️ Borderline (33.7%) |
| 2-bedroom (90m²) | €1,014/mo | €1,239/mo | ❌ No (50.6%) |

A median-income household cannot afford a 2-bedroom apartment in Berlin without exceeding the 30% threshold. With utilities, even a 1-bedroom crosses the line.

## The geography of inequality

Berlin's 12 districts tell a story of spatial inequality:

- **Neukölln**: €9.79/m² — 64% of housing stock in einfache (simple) Lage
- **Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf**: €12.80/m² — 58% in gute (good) Lage

A 60m² apartment costs €587/month in Neukölln vs €768 in Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf. That's €181/month — €2,172/year — for the same square meters in a different postcode.

## What this means

The Mietpreisbremse didn't brake Berlin's rent growth. It may have even contributed to it — by creating a two-tier market where new builds and modernized units escape regulation entirely, driving up the averages that the Mietspiegel itself tracks.

For Berliners, the practical reality is: **don't move.** The gap between existing and new rents is now 35%, and it's growing. The city's rental market is bifurcating into those who got in early and everyone else.
